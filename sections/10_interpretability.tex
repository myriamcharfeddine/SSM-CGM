\section{Interpretability: Model Reliance, Failure Modes, and Local Forecast Explanations}
\label{sec:interp}

\subsection{Motivation}
\label{subsec:interp_motivation}

Understanding which inputs drive the frozen base model and where it fails is
important for three reasons.
First, the residual-current target means the model can appear well-calibrated
simply by tracking recent glucose; it is necessary to verify that it captures
more than a persistence signal.
Second, Study~1 (Section~\ref{sec:study1_cf_audit}) established that meal-proxy
signals have no causal interpretability on AI-READI; the interpretability
analysis here shows what the model \emph{does} use, not what it should use.
Third, the Study~2 correction head
(Section~\ref{subsec:interp_head}) is interpretable by construction: its Ridge
coefficients directly expose which causal features drive the correction at
each forecast horizon.

The most defensible interpretability results for this AI-READI run are the
modality attribution, temporal-lag attribution, failure-mode analysis, and
the correction-head coefficient profiles.
The proxy scenario branch is not currently interpretable as a physiological
what-if mechanism: prediction deltas are near zero, and
\texttt{predmeal\_flag} is unavailable in the audited future horizon.
No insulin-on-board equivalent is available in AI-READI, and no insulin
causal interpretation should be drawn.

All attribution results below are based on leave-one-modality-out ablation
(global) or MES hidden-attention weights (temporal lag) computed on
58 test-split triggered anchor windows from non-insulin participants.
Correction-head interpretability uses the Ridge coefficient tables in
\texttt{outputs/study2\_interpretability/tables/}.
No numbers are invented; all values are read from disk artifacts.

\subsection{Base-Model Input Attribution by Modality}
\label{subsec:interp_modality}

Fig.~\ref{fig:interp_global} summarises global modality attribution computed
via leave-one-modality-out ablation on 58 test-split triggered anchor windows
(non-insulin participants).
Attribution is measured as the mean absolute change in the 12-step forecast
when the named modality is replaced by its population mean.

Table~\ref{tab:interp_modality} lists fractions of total influence.
\textbf{Glucose history} accounts for 37.2\% of input influence -- the dominant
single modality, as expected for a CGM-forecasting task.
\textbf{Static clinical} (age, BMI, study-enrolment variables) adds 12.4\%;
\textbf{data quality} and \textbf{site/cohort} together add 18.7\%.
The latter two modalities capture data-collection artefacts and recruitment-site
effects rather than physiological signals; their combined 18.7\% influence is
a caution that the base model has partially learned non-physiological
population structure.
\textbf{Heart rate}, \textbf{medication metadata}, and \textbf{steps/activity}
each contribute 4--7\%.
\textbf{Meal proxy (diagnostic only)} registers 0.0\%: consistent with Study~1,
the base model derives no usable signal from the available meal-proxy channel.

% ── Subsection 2: Temporal lag attribution ────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Temporal Lag Attribution (MES Hidden Attention)}
\label{subsec:interp_lag}

The Mamba encoder exposes a mixture-of-experts (MES) hidden-attention weight
over input lags.
Fig.~\ref{fig:interp_temporal} plots the mean attention weight as a function
of lag for each modality.
For glucose history, 79\% of the attention weight falls on lag 0 (current
glucose; fractional weight 0.206) and lag 5~min (weight 0.145), with
a long, slowly decaying tail out to $\sim$4~hours.
This pattern is consistent with Mamba's selective-state design: the recurrent
state efficiently compresses the recent glucose trajectory, placing the highest
explicit attention on the most recent observations.

% ── Subsection 3: Correction-head coefficients ────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Correction-Head Coefficient Profiles}
\label{subsec:interp_head}

The Study~2 final head is a set of 12 per-horizon Ridge regression models
(separate up- and down-head).
The Ridge structure makes coefficients directly interpretable as the
standardised influence of each causal feature on the correction applied
to the frozen-base median.

\paragraph{Dominant terms.}
Table~\ref{tab:interp_coef} lists the five largest-magnitude standardised
coefficients for trigger\_up at five selected horizons.
At $h=1$ (5~min), \textbf{current residual} $r_t$ has the highest positive
weight (0.80), reflecting that the immediately available one-step error is
the strongest signal for short-horizon correction.
The lagged residuals $r_{t-1}$ and $r_{t-2}$ carry large \emph{negative}
weights ($-0.66$, $-0.65$): these encode mean reversion -- if the last two
residuals were also positive (a sustained up-trigger), the expected correction
is tempered because the base model tends to mean-revert across anchors.
\textbf{Slope$_{15}$} (15-min glucose rate of change) is the second largest
positive term at $h=1$ (0.71) and remains in the top~5 at all horizons;
its importance is consistent with the ablation result that slope+curvature
is a distinct informative group.

\paragraph{Horizon dependence.}
Coefficient magnitudes scale roughly linearly with horizon up to $h=6$
(30~min) and then plateau.
At $h=6$ and beyond, \textbf{interval width} $w_t$ (the frozen-base
90th--10th quantile spread) enters the top~5, indicating that the
correction head uses the model's own uncertainty as a scaling factor for
large-horizon adjustments.
\textbf{Current glucose} $G_t$ becomes the fourth-largest term at $h \geq 6$,
reflecting the mean-reverting nature of glucose dynamics over longer windows.

\paragraph{Up vs down head.}
Fig.~\ref{fig:interp_head_updown} overlays the standardised coefficients
of the trigger\_up and trigger\_down heads at $h=1$.
The largest asymmetry is in $r_{t-1}$: the down-head places a much smaller
negative weight on $r_{t-1}$ ($-0.034$) than the up-head ($-0.664$),
because downward triggers tend to be sharper and shorter than upward excursions
in this dataset.
Current glucose $G_t$ switches sign between heads: positive for up ($+0.241$)
and negative for down ($-0.437$), reflecting that hypoglycaemia corrections
are larger when glucose is already low.

\subsection{Failure-Mode Analysis}
\label{subsec:interp_failure}

Fig.~\ref{fig:interp_failure} and Table~\ref{tab:interp_failure} show
correction gain (base MAE $-$ corrected MAE, mg/dL) broken down by
conditioning dimensions on the test split (39{,}698 triggered anchors,
204 participants).
All CIs are participant-clustered.

\paragraph{By trigger direction.}
Trigger-up windows gain $+0.305$~mg/dL on average; trigger-down windows
gain only $+0.026$~mg/dL.
The asymmetry is consistent with the coefficient analysis: the up-head
is better calibrated to the dominant pattern in this non-insulin dataset,
where upward glucose excursions are both more frequent and larger.

\paragraph{By residual magnitude.}
The correction head is most valuable when the base model's one-step error
is large.
Residuals in $[10,20]$~mg/dL gain $+0.937$~mg/dL; residuals $>20$~mg/dL
gain $+0.995$~mg/dL.
One category is harmful: residuals $< -20$~mg/dL lose $-0.603$~mg/dL
(77 anchors, 50 participants).
This is the head's primary failure mode: extreme downward surprises where
the base model underestimated a rapid drop are over-corrected in the
negative direction.

\paragraph{By interval width.}
Quartile Q4 of the frozen-base uncertainty interval (widest, highest
uncertainty) gains $+0.435$~mg/dL; Q1 (narrowest) gains $-0.022$~mg/dL
(essentially zero, slight harm).
The head should not be applied, or should be penalised, at very narrow
prediction intervals where the base model is already confident.

\paragraph{By slope.}
Fast-rising glucose ($> 2$~mg/dL/min) gains $+0.590$~mg/dL; flat windows
gain only $+0.024$~mg/dL; mild-down windows gain $-0.001$~mg/dL.
The head adds the most value during active glucose rises and is nearly
neutral during stable glucose.

\paragraph{By study group.}
Healthy participants gain $-0.019$~mg/dL (negligible, essentially no
correction benefit).
Oral-medication-controlled participants gain $+0.442$~mg/dL; insulin-dependent
participants gain $+0.434$~mg/dL (8 participants, 1{,}498 anchors -- interpret
with caution due to the small participant count).
The pattern is consistent: the correction head is most useful where the
base model is most challenged -- non-healthy participants with more variable
glucose dynamics.

\subsection{Limitations of the Interpretability Analysis}
\label{subsec:interp_limitations}

\paragraph{Attribution sample size.}
The global modality attribution is based on 58 test-split triggered anchor
windows.
While 58 anchors span all 204 non-insulin test participants, the sample is small
relative to the full 39{,}698 triggered test anchor set.
Aggregate ablation results are directionally reliable but individual modality
fractions carry uncertainty.

\paragraph{MES attention interpretation.}
The temporal lag attribution uses hidden-attention weights from a specific Mamba
encoder head (head 0).
These weights reflect the model's information routing, not necessarily the
biological salience of each lag.
They should not be over-interpreted as evidence that, for example,
glucose at 60-minute lookback is physiologically unimportant.

\paragraph{Local attribution proxy.}
Local episode panels (Figs.~\ref{fig:interp_ep1}--\ref{fig:interp_ep4}) use
Ridge coefficient contributions (feature value times raw-scale coefficient)
as a proxy for local modality attribution.
Exact local modality attribution for the base model would require repeated
forward passes with modality ablation on each episode individually.
The coefficient proxy is interpretable but does not separate base-model
reliance from correction-head decisions.

\paragraph{Meal proxy attribution.}
The 0.0\% attributed influence for the meal proxy channel is consistent with
Study~1 but does not prove that the base model has never used meal-proxy
information during training.
The channel may have carried transient signal during early training epochs
before the dead-channel failure was identified and corrected
(Section~\ref{sec:study1_cf_audit}).

\paragraph{Site and phenotype effects.}
Modality attribution aggregates across three clinical sites (UAB, UCSD, UW)
and four study groups.
Site/cohort accounts for 8.1\% of influence; this may reflect domain-shift
learning rather than physiological generalisation.
Per-site attribution profiles are not currently available but would be
informative for deployment to new sites.
